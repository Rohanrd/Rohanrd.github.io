Research

I work at the intersection of artificial intelligence, computer vision, and healthcare. The question that has shaped my academic and industrial career is simple to state and hard to answer: how do we build AI systems that truly understand complex visual information, and can be trusted to act on that understanding in real clinical settings?

My path to this question started in computer vision and image analysis more broadly — image restoration, registration, segmentation, fusion, video analysis, and deep learning. During my PhD at the Indian Institute of Technology Delhi, that foundation sharpened into a specific focus: AI for neurosurgical skill assessment. I built datasets, computer vision methods, and learning frameworks to understand what happens during surgery and to objectively evaluate how well it was performed.

Today, as a Senior AI Scientist at GE HealthCare, that question has evolved again — toward trustworthy and explainable computer vision AI. My work now centers on understanding model behavior, tracing the sources of unreliability, and building approaches that make AI systems more dependable and interpretable in healthcare.

Seen as a whole, my research traces a single line: from understanding images, to understanding and objectively evaluating surgical performance, to building AI systems that can be trusted, adapted, and put to active use by clinicians.

Research Journey

I don't think of these as separate projects so much as one progression — through increasingly difficult forms of visual understanding, toward increasingly consequential healthcare applications.

Early Research

Computer Vision & Medical Imaging

My earliest work built a foundation in computer vision and image analysis: image restoration, motion deblurring, registration, fusion, segmentation, video summarization, and feature selection. Each project was, at its core, about pulling meaningful signal out of imperfect visual data.

That foundation turned out to be exactly what I needed for the far harder visual problems waiting in healthcare and surgery.

Doctoral Research · IIT Delhi

AI for Surgical Skill Assessment

My PhD centered on one problem: automated, objective assessment of neurosurgical skill. The goal was to replace subjective evaluation with AI systems that could understand what was happening during surgery and quantify how well it was done — which meant tackling it from both ends.

One thread taught AI to understand what is actually happening during a procedure, through temporal action segmentation of neuroendoscopic video and instance segmentation of surgical instruments — under conditions that are typical in medicine but punishing for most vision models: scarce annotated data, fine-grained visual structures, and high temporal variability. That led to new methods in video augmentation, curriculum learning, and query initialization for transformer-based instance segmentation.

The other thread asked the question that actually matters to a trainee: how well am I performing? That produced automated assessment methods for microsuturing, microdrilling, and endoscopic neurosurgical skills, built on purpose-made datasets and AI frameworks. Its clearest outcome was NeuroMentor, an AI-based system for surgical skills evaluation and training that brings objective assessment directly into surgical education — and the experience that shaped a core piece of my research philosophy: algorithmic advances matter most when they end up as tools that solve real problems for clinicians and trainees, not just results in a paper.

Current Research · GE HealthCare

From “Can AI Understand?” to “Can We Trust AI?”

My current work builds on that experience but shifts the central question. In healthcare, accurate predictions are not enough on their own — we also need to know when and why a prediction can be trusted. As a Senior AI Scientist at GE HealthCare, I focus on reliability, explainability, model behavior, and failure modes in computer vision AI, working on methods that can identify, explain, and ultimately reduce the sources of unreliability in both AI systems and the data used to train them.

My most recent work extends this into a broader shift: from AI systems that simply predict to systems that can be configured, evaluated, and used interactively by the clinician on the other end. Two recent patent filings reflect this direction — one on prompt-driven creation of specialized medical imaging models, the other on AI-guided ultrasound acquisition. Both point toward the same idea: AI that adapts to the clinical requirement in front of it and actively assists the user, rather than sitting passively behind a prediction.

Selected Research

A few key steps along the way — from understanding surgical activity, to objectively evaluating surgical performance, to improving the reliability of medical AI more broadly.

ICIP 2023 · Surgical Skill Assessment

From Feline Classification to Skills Evaluation

A multitask learning framework that evaluates neurosurgical micro-suturing skill directly from the final surgical outcome — designed to hold up against small, long-tailed datasets and wide variation in magnification and suture size.

[Project]

MICCAI 2024 · Surgical Video Understanding

VideoCutMix

A video-specific augmentation and curriculum-learning method for temporal segmentation of surgical video when training data is scarce, introduced alongside the Neuro-endoscopic Trainee Simulator (NETS) dataset.

[Project]

MICCAI 2023 · Surgical Instrument Segmentation

Learnable Query Initialization

A study of transformer-based instance segmentation for fine-grained surgical instruments that traces a key limitation to query initialization, and fixes it with a learnable query proposal mechanism.

[Project]

Current Research · Trustworthy AI

RELAX: Reliability Enhancement by Limiting Adverse Exertions

A mechanism for improving the reliability of AI systems by limiting adverse exertions during training — accepted at Nature Scientific Reports.

Co-authors: Raghunathan Selvam, Prasad Sudhakar Murthy, Rahul Venkataramani, and Chandan Aladahalli.

MIDL · Short Paper · Trustworthy AI

MIRROR: Multi-model Inference and Regional Reasoning for Recognizing Annotation Errors

A framework using multi-model inference and regional reasoning to catch annotation errors in medical imaging — part of a broader interest in finding where unreliability enters medical AI pipelines in the first place.

Accepted as a short paper at MIDL.
